#Machine Learning and AI
Research on machine learning and artificial intelligence for environmental exposure modelling, exposome research, and epidemiological analysis. Includes methods for integrating heterogeneous environmental and health data, modelling complex exposure mixtures, reconstructing exposures, and improving prediction across populations, places, and time. Particular interest in multimodal learning, foundation models with epidemiological applications, uncertainty quantification, interpretable modelling, and approaches that work with limited or imperfectly measured data. Include methodological advances with a clear pathway to environmental epidemiology even when a completed health application is not yet available. Prioritise meaningful improvements over established methods, external validation, reproducibility, and benchmarks that measure capabilities relevant to environmental and population health research. Also includes substantive perspectives on the scientific opportunities, limitations, bias, and responsible use of AI in epidemiology. Deprioritise generic clinical prediction, hospital applications, and medical imaging without an environmental exposure connection.

#Geospatial Foundation Models
Research on geospatial artificial intelligence (GeoAI), with particular emphasis on the development, evaluation, and application of geospatial and Earth observation foundation models. Includes large-scale pretrained models, self-supervised learning, transferable spatial and spatiotemporal representations, satellite image time-series models, and multimodal models integrating optical, radar, LiDAR, hyperspectral, or thermal observations with maps, street-level imagery, text, climate data, or other geospatial information. Particular interest in reusable geospatial embeddings and models supporting environmental exposure assessment, exposome research, environmental epidemiology, biodiversity monitoring, and the mapping of land use, vegetation, forest disturbances, wildfire, air pollution, and urban environments. Include foundational methodological advances, pretraining datasets, embedding datasets, and benchmarks even when they do not yet include an epidemiological application, provided they offer a clear pathway to environmental or population-level spatial analysis. Prioritise transfer across regions, time periods, sensors, and spatial resolutions; performance with limited labels; fine-tuning and zero-shot or few-shot learning; uncertainty quantification; and reproducible comparisons with simpler methods. Also includes critical evaluations of spatial and temporal data leakage, geographic bias, out-of-distribution generalisation, and whether pretrained representations improve downstream environmental modelling. Deprioritise routine applications of supervised machine learning to a single mapping task without a substantive methodological contribution, and generic language models or medical imaging models without a geospatial or environmental connection.

#Causal Epidemiology
Causal epidemiology research estimating the health effects of environmental exposures, with particular interest in spatial and spatiotemporal exposure patterns, time-varying exposures, and exposure mixtures. Includes natural experiments, difference-in-differences, interrupted time series, regression discontinuity, instrumental variables, target trial emulation, longitudinal g-methods, targeted learning, causal forests, and double/debiased machine learning. Covers methodological developments and applied studies addressing confounding, selection bias, exposure measurement error, spatial dependence, interference or spillover effects, and transportability across populations. Prioritise clearly defined causal questions and estimands, credible identification strategies, explicit assumptions, and informative sensitivity analyses. Include strong design-based studies even when they do not use advanced machine learning. Exclude purely associational analyses without a substantive causal framework; causal language or the use of a particular algorithm alone is insufficient.

#Remote Sensing in Epidemiology
Research using satellite, airborne, drone, or other remote sensing observations to develop, validate, or apply measures of environmental exposure relevant to epidemiology and population health. Includes air pollution, temperature and heat, vegetation and green space, land use, urban form, agricultural activity, and other environmental stressors or protective factors. Covers both studies linking remotely sensed exposures to health outcomes and upstream exposure products or methodological advances with clear epidemiological relevance, even without a health-outcome analysis. Particular interest in spatial and spatiotemporal exposure assessment, integration with ground monitoring and health datasets, historical exposure reconstruction, and improved spatial or temporal resolution. Prioritise validation, uncertainty and measurement error, geographic transferability, and the implications of exposure assignment, residential mobility, and spatial aggregation for epidemiological analyses. Deprioritise routine remote sensing classifications without a clear contribution to exposure assessment or population health.

#Air Pollution Exposure and Health
Environmental epidemiology research examining the health effects and population exposure patterns of outdoor and indoor air pollution. Includes PM2.5, PM10, ultrafine particles, black carbon, NO₂, O₃, SO₂, VOCs, and pollutant mixtures, with attention to source-specific pollution from traffic, agriculture, industry, and household combustion. Covers short- and long-term effects on respiratory, cardiovascular, neurological, metabolic, reproductive, and other health outcomes. Includes cohort, time-series, case-crossover, spatial, and quasi-experimental studies, as well as exposure assessment using land-use regression, dispersion and chemical transport modelling, sensor networks, and personal monitoring. Particular interest in exposure measurement error, pollutant composition and sources, susceptible populations, environmental justice, and socioeconomic disparities in exposure and health burden. Prioritise robust epidemiological evidence and substantive improvements in exposure assessment. Include other environmental exposures when studied jointly with air pollution or when they modify its health effects.

#Spatial Pesticide Exposure Assessment
Studies developing, validating, or applying spatial and spatiotemporal methods to estimate human exposure to pesticides. Particular interest in GIS-based approaches combining pesticide application records, crop maps, agricultural land use, meteorology, residential locations, and other environmental datasets. Includes proximity and buffer-based metrics, spray drift, volatilisation, atmospheric transport, environmental fate, and contamination of soil or water when relevant to population exposure. Covers historical exposure reconstruction, cumulative exposure and mixtures, and integration with epidemiological datasets. Prioritise validation against environmental measurements, personal monitoring, or biomonitoring, along with uncertainty assessment and comparisons between exposure estimation methods. Include occupational and take-home pathways when they contribute to spatial assessment of community or household exposure. Deprioritise laboratory toxicology, pesticide efficacy, and purely agronomic studies without a substantive exposure assessment contribution.

#Wildfire Smoke Exposure and Health
Wildfire research focused on human exposure and health, including both epidemiological studies and the development of exposure estimates suitable for population health research. Particular interest in wildfire-specific PM2.5, smoke plume detection and transport, source attribution, historical exposure reconstruction, and integration of remote sensing, atmospheric models, monitoring networks, and GIS. Includes short- and long-term health effects, repeated smoke episodes, susceptible populations, inequalities in exposure, and the effectiveness of exposure-reduction measures. Cover methodological advances in estimating smoke exposure and distinguishing wildfire pollution from other sources even when no health analysis is included. Also includes other wildfire-related hazards when directly linked to human exposure or health. Prioritise validated exposure methods and robust epidemiological evidence; deprioritise fire behaviour, combustion, and ecosystem studies without a clear population exposure or health connection.

#Forest Disturbance and Change
Selective coverage of major advances in understanding and monitoring forest disturbance, including deforestation, degradation, wildfire, drought, pests, storms, and forest recovery. This is a secondary interest: prioritise landmark studies, broad syntheses, influential reviews, major global or continental assessments, and widely reusable datasets or monitoring approaches that substantially change understanding of forest change. Include especially consequential findings on disturbance drivers, compound disturbances, ecosystem resilience, biodiversity, climate, or human health. Remote sensing and spatial modelling studies are relevant when they enable a substantial advance at broad scales or establish a broadly useful capability. Ecological studies do not need a health connection to qualify. Deprioritise incremental algorithm comparisons, routine mapping updates, narrowly local case studies, and specialised technical details unless they demonstrate an unusually important and generalisable finding. A paper should offer a clear reason for a researcher outside forest science to pay attention; publication venue alone is not sufficient.

#Biodiversity Change and Global Data
Selective coverage of major biodiversity discoveries, assessments, and data resources, intended to maintain broad awareness rather than follow the specialist literature comprehensively. Prioritise landmark global or continental analyses, major syntheses, influential reviews, and widely reusable biodiversity datasets or infrastructures that substantially improve understanding of biodiversity patterns, loss, recovery, or ecosystem change. Include consequential advances that overcome major limitations in biodiversity data coverage or reliability, without prioritising routine technical refinements. Also include strong, broadly relevant evidence connecting biodiversity with environmental exposures, infectious disease, or other human health outcomes; such studies may be local or regional when their implications extend beyond the study setting. Deprioritise routine species distribution modelling, isolated species inventories, small dataset updates, narrow taxonomic studies, and incremental methodological comparisons. A paper should have substantial scientific or societal implications that can be explained to a non-specialist; publication venue alone is not sufficient.
